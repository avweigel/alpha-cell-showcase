How it works

From gray to understanding — in one movie

A focused ion beam shaves the sample away a few nanometers at a time while an electron microscope photographs each freshly exposed face; machine learning then gives every voxel a name. Watch raw electron microscopy become labeled, 3D, measurable structure.

Raw FIB-SEM data with the machine-learning segmentation overlaid: every color is an organelle class, identified automatically for every voxel in the volume.

Structures counted across whole tissues

Once a structure can be recognized automatically, it can be counted everywhere — turning “does this vary across the tissue?” into a lookup. Nuclear pores in liver 2026, in preparation, plasmodesmata in plants, and the biggest mitochondria you have ever seen.

Nuclear pores, counted across a tissue

What you see: each nucleus rendered from the EM data, and every bright dot on it is one nuclear pore, the channel that controls traffic in and out of the nucleus. Pores are stereotyped enough that a model finds all of them automatically across a whole tissue volume. In this liver-zonation dataset, counting them per nucleus shows transport capacity following the central→portal metabolic gradient.

Plasmodesmata — same idea, different kingdom

The channels connecting plant cells, volume-rendered from FIB-SEM of leaf tissue. Mapping their density across healthy vs gall-infected leaves turns a plant-development question into a counting problem.
The readout: every nucleus in the volume, colored by its pore count, laid out along the acinus from central vein to portal vein. Color = how many pores each nucleus has.
Plasmodesmata density mapped across a whole leaf section — gall-infected tissue, with healthy tissue as the comparison.

Every dot is a nuclear pore

One nucleus rendered from the EM with each pore found automatically. Scaled up: 16.8 million pores across 26,104 nuclei in 16 datasets — the largest survey of nuclear pore organization assembled to date (manuscript in preparation, 2026).

The honest slide

What this data cannot capture

Volume EM shows every membrane and organelle, but it is blind in specific, well-understood ways — and each blind spot is exactly where another part of Alpha Cell comes in.

No molecular identity

EM contrast shows structure, not names: a vesicle full of protein X looks like a vesicle full of protein Y. That is what the Human Protein Atlas and multiplexed light microscopy bring — who is where — and why Alpha Cell registers imaging modalities onto the same cells.

One frozen moment

Every volume is a snapshot of a fixed or frozen cell — no motion, no before and after. Dynamics come from live light microscopy, and building the temporal reference is its own phase of the program (Cell Time).

Preparation leaves fingerprints

Chemical fixation subtly reshapes membranes; rapid freezing preserves them better but is harder to scale. This is measurable — the deep-dive section below quantifies exactly how preservation choices change what you see — and it is why acquisition protocols have to be benchmarked, not assumed.

Structure below the voxel

At 4–8 nm you see the membrane, not the molecules in it. Protein-scale structure in a native state is cryo-ET territory — the laser-phase-plate TEM planned for the program — with FIB-SEM providing the cellular context around it.

No single microscope builds a virtual cell. The point of Alpha Cell's Space phase is to combine these views — volume EM for geometry and context, light microscopy for identity and time, cryo-ET for molecular structure — into one registered, model-ready reference.

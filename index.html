Into the cell

Every cell is a crowded, three-dimensional world — and for the first time we can map it, structure by structure. This is a corner of a real cell. Let's take one piece of mouse liver and zoom in, about a million times.

Meanwhile, elsewhere in nature

The same trick works on almost anything you can freeze. We once imaged the entire biting needle of a mosquito — every cell of the organ, end to end.
Two minutes through a mosquito's stylet, from raw images to the full 3D reconstruction.
How do we even see this? (60 seconds)

A beam of charged particles shaves the frozen sample — a few billionths of a meter at a time — and after each shave, an electron microscope photographs the freshly exposed face. Thousands of photos stack into a 3D image. Then AI, trained by human experts, gives every dot in that image a name: this is mitochondrion, this is nucleus, this is membrane.

Watch it happen for real: raw microscope images, and then every structure identified, in color.

Chapter 5 · Now take it apart

One scan, every piecereal data

Everything on this journey came out of one dataset — and because every point in it is labeled, that dataset works like a kit. Ask for only the blood vessels, and you get them. Only the nuclei. Every mitochondrion — all 1.3 million of them. Watch the same liver block from chapter 2 come apart, piece by piece:

The liver dataset, disassembled: the blood supply, the bile drainage network, 1,897 nuclei, 1.3 million mitochondria, 49,801 lipid droplets, ~27,000 peroxisomes — every piece lifted straight out of the same segmentation, nothing drawn by hand.
What do you do with the pieces?

Once a structure is extracted it stops being a picture and becomes a list — objects you can count, measure, and compare. How many mitochondria does a cell keep near a big vein versus far from one? How much fat does each cell store? How far is any point in the tissue from the nearest blood supply? Those are the questions this data was made to answer. And the precision holds all the way down: at the very bottom of the scale even the gap between two neighboring cells — a few tens of nanometers — is a measurement, sharp enough to show that fast-frozen tissue keeps cells measurably closer together than chemical preservation. Knowing that is how we choose the methods that show cells as they truly are.

Chapter 6 · Now run it backwards

From seeing to understanding — the virtual cell

The journey you just took was through structure: a snapshot of one frozen moment, with no protein names attached. Alpha Cell works on the missing dimensions. Space: where each of a cell's thousands of proteins lives, building on twenty years of the Human Protein Atlas. Time: how a living cell changes, followed through its whole division cycle. The measurements start in U2OS, a human cell line researchers know well, then move to stem cells. Together they become training data for AI models of the cell. The aim: ask the model what happens when a drug arrives or a gene breaks, get an answer in molecular terms, and check it against reality.

The loop scientists call "lab in the loop": each round of measurements improves the model, and the model tells us which measurement matters most next. Alpha Cell is SciLifeLab's flagship program, coordinated with the Human Protein Atlas.
Being honest: what one microscope cannot see

The data on this page shows structure in breathtaking detail — but it is blind in specific ways, and each blind spot is exactly why Alpha Cell combines methods. The images name no proteins — the Human Protein Atlas and light microscopy add the who's-who. Each volume is one frozen instant — live-cell microscopy adds time. Below our sharpest pixels sit the molecules themselves — cryo-electron microscopy goes there. And preparing a sample always leaves fingerprints — which is why we measure our own methods as carefully as our cells (that's the chart in Chapter 5). No single microscope builds a virtual cell. All of them together just might.

For the scientists: the fine print

Data: isotropic FIB-SEM at 4–8 nm voxel size, Janelia CellMap / OpenOrganelle collections (mouse liver, kidney, heart, cortex; HeLa, macrophage and other cultured cells; CellMap Segmentation Challenge ground truth). The whole-cell model was meshed from the public COSEM segmentations of jrc_hela-2. The membrane measurements (contact gaps, curvature, protrusions; chemical vs rapid-cryo comparison), the mosquito stylet reconstruction, the liver zonation acinus, and the nuclear-pore survey are from manuscripts in preparation — please don't redistribute those outside the group.
